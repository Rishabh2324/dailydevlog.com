## Why this matters

"Our model is 99% accurate" sounds great. It can also describe a model that never catches a single fraud case.
Precision and recall are the words teams use to talk about what a model gets wrong, and they come back later for
search and RAG quality (lesson 3.2.3).

By the end of this lesson, you'll know what accuracy, precision and recall mean, and why picking one is a product
decision.

## The idea

When a model says yes or no, it can be wrong in two ways:

- A **false positive**: it says "yes" but the answer is "no". A normal email lands in spam.
- A **false negative**: it says "no" but the answer is "yes". A spam email reaches your inbox.

Three common scores:

- **Accuracy:** out of all answers, how many were right.
- **Precision:** when the model says "yes", how often is it right? (Few false alarms.)
- **Recall:** out of all the real "yes" cases, how many did the model catch? (Few missed cases.)

**F1 score** combines precision and recall into one number.

```text
                     really spam   really not spam
model says spam      caught ✓      false alarm ✗
model says not spam  missed ✗      correct ✓

precision = caught / (caught + false alarms)
recall    = caught / (caught + missed)
```

This grid is called a **confusion matrix**.

## A real-life example

Think of an **airport security scanner**.

- If it's tuned for **high recall**, it beeps at almost every dangerous item, but also at belts, keys and coins.
  Lots of false alarms, few misses.
- If it's tuned for **high precision**, it only beeps when it's very sure. Fewer annoyed travelers, but some
  dangerous items get through.

Airports choose high recall, because a miss is far worse than a bag check.

Now the accuracy trap. Suppose only 1 in 100 card payments is fraud. A "model" that always says "not fraud" is
**99% accurate** and catches **zero** fraud. Its recall is 0%. That's why accuracy alone is not enough when one case
is rare.

## How it works

1. **Run the model on labeled test data** it hasn't seen (lesson 1.1.10).
2. **Count the four outcomes**: correct yes, false alarm, missed, correct no.
3. **Compute the scores** from those counts.
4. **Adjust the threshold.** Many models give a probability, like "0.7 chance of fraud". You choose the cut-off.
   A lower threshold catches more fraud (higher recall) but raises more false alarms (lower precision).
5. **Decide based on cost.** Which mistake hurts more for your product? That answer picks the threshold.

## Where you'll see it

- **Spam filters, fraud checks and content moderation** are all tuned with this trade-off.
- **Medical tests** use the same idea under the names sensitivity (recall) and specificity.
- **Search and RAG** use precision and recall for "did we find the right documents?" (lesson 3.2.3).
- **Model cards and papers** report F1, precision and recall for classification tasks.

## Common confusions

- **Precision vs recall:** precision is about the model's "yes" answers being trustworthy; recall is about not
  missing real cases.
- **There is no best metric in general.** The right one depends on which mistake is more expensive for your users.
- **High accuracy can be meaningless** when one class is rare, like fraud or rare diseases.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Share of all answers that were right |
| Precision | Share of "yes" answers that were really yes |
| Recall | Share of real "yes" cases the model caught |
| F1 score | One number combining precision and recall |
| Confusion matrix | A table of the four right and wrong outcomes |
| Threshold | The probability cut-off that turns a score into yes or no |
