## Why this matters

Why did AI companies spend billions building ever bigger models? Because they could predict it would work.
Scaling laws are that prediction. They also explain today's trends: smaller models trained on more data, and
"reasoning" models that think longer before answering.

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to explain what scaling laws say and what they don't.

## The idea

**Scaling laws** describe a pattern researchers found: a language model's loss (lesson 1.1.6) goes down smoothly
and predictably as you increase three things:

- **Parameters**: the size of the model.
- **Data**: how many tokens it trains on.
- **Compute**: how much GPU work training uses.

The improvement has **diminishing returns**. Each time you make things 10 times bigger, you get a smaller gain than
last time. But the gain is predictable, so labs can test ideas on small models and forecast how a huge one will do.

## A real-life example

Think of **studying for an exam**. The first 10 hours of study raise your score a lot. The next 10 hours help, but
less. The next 100 hours help a little more. You can roughly predict your score from your study hours, and you can
see that each extra hour is worth less.

But study time isn't the only thing that matters. **Good study material** also counts. And someone who studied a
little less, but has good notes and **takes more time to think during the exam**, can beat someone who just studied
more. AI scaling went through the same three stages:

1. **Bigger models.** OpenAI's 2020 study showed loss improves predictably with size (Kaplan et al., 2020).
2. **Balance size with data.** DeepMind found many models were too big for how much data they saw. Their
   Chinchilla model, smaller but trained on more data, beat the much larger Gopher using a similar compute budget
   (Hoffmann et al., 2022). A rule of thumb from that work is about **20 training tokens per parameter**.
3. **More thinking at answer time.** Newer models spend extra compute while answering, which can beat simply using a
   bigger model on some tasks (Snell et al., 2024). Lesson 1.4.5 covers these reasoning models.

## How it works

1. **Researchers train many small models** of different sizes on different amounts of data.
2. **They plot loss against size** and find a smooth curve.
3. **They extend the curve** to predict how a model 100 times bigger would perform, before paying for it.
4. **They choose a size and data amount** that give the best result for their compute budget.
5. **In practice, companies often train smaller models on far more data** than the balanced amount. A smaller model
   is cheaper to run for millions of users. Meta trained Llama 3 8B on over 15 trillion tokens, far beyond 20 per
   parameter, and reported it kept improving (Meta, 2024).

## Where you'll see it

- **Model announcements** often mention parameter count, training tokens and compute.
- **Small models getting better:** new small models can match older, much larger ones.
- **"Test-time compute" or "thinking" modes** in chat apps are the newest scaling direction.
- **Debates about "the end of scaling"** are about whether these curves keep holding, and whether enough good data
  exists.

## Common confusions

- **Scaling laws aren't laws of nature.** They are patterns fitted to experiments, and they can change with new
  methods or data.
- **Lower loss doesn't guarantee a specific skill.** A model can improve overall while one ability stays weak.
- **Bigger isn't automatically better for your product.** A smaller, well-trained model is often faster, cheaper and
  good enough.

<Callout type="note">
  Exact numbers from scaling law papers vary between studies. Treat "20 tokens per parameter" as a rough guide, not
  a rule.
</Callout>

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scaling laws | Predictable improvement as parameters, data and compute grow |
| Diminishing returns | Each extra step up gives a smaller gain |
| Compute budget | How much training compute you can afford |
| Compute-optimal | The best split between model size and data for a budget |
| Test-time compute | Extra compute a model spends while answering |

## Module check

You've finished Module 1.1. Try answering these in a sentence or two before opening the answers.

1. What's the difference between writing rules and training a model?
2. What are parameters, and what does "7B" tell you?
3. What does a loss function do, and how does gradient descent use it?
4. What is overfitting, and why must the test set stay separate?
5. Why can a 99% accurate fraud model be useless?

<Answer label="Answers">
  1. With rules, you write the logic. With a model, you give examples and correct answers, and training finds the
     logic (lesson 1.1.2).
  2. Parameters are the numbers a model learns, mostly weights and biases. "7B" means about 7 billion of them,
     roughly 14 GB of memory at 16-bit (lesson 1.1.5).
  3. The loss function scores how wrong the model is. Gradient descent keeps nudging the parameters in the
     direction that lowers that score (lessons 1.1.6 and 1.1.7).
  4. Overfitting is memorizing training examples instead of learning the pattern. If test data leaks into training,
     the final score no longer shows how the model does on new data (lesson 1.1.10).
  5. If fraud is 1% of payments, a model that always says "not fraud" is 99% accurate but catches nothing. Recall
     shows the problem (lesson 1.1.11).
</Answer>
