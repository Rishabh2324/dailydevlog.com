## Why this matters

Your team got an AI coding assistant, and everyone expects features to ship faster. But how much of your last
feature was actually spent writing code?

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to split one feature's time into work and waiting, and spot where AI
could really help.

## The idea

**Speeding up one step can only save the time that step takes.** This is Amdahl's law (Amdahl, 1967).

If coding is 10% of a feature's time and AI makes coding twice as fast, the feature ships only 5% sooner. Most of
the time usually goes to waiting: for answers, reviews, CI and deploys.

```text
One feature, 48 working hours

 work    ██████ 15.5h   (coding: 5h)
 waiting █████████████ 32.5h
```

This is common. In Atlassian's 2025 survey, developers said only about 16% of their time goes to coding (Atlassian,
2025). And feelings mislead: in one study, developers using AI were 19% slower but felt 20% faster (Becker et al.,
METR, 2025). So measure, don't guess.

## How to do it

1. Pick one recent, normal feature. **(you decide)**
2. List its events with times: ticket created, questions, PR, reviews, CI, merge, deploy.
3. Ask AI to sort the hours into work and waiting, in a table.
4. Check the math yourself, and fix any mistakes.
5. Look at the biggest wait and decide what to change. **(you decide)**

## Example

ShipLog is the team changelog app used in this course. SHIP-142 adds a public changelog page. The event log is
invented; the AI output is real (Claude Sonnet, 2026-09-17, trimmed).

```text
Team hours are 09:00-17:00, Mon-Fri. Here are the events
for one feature. Split the working hours into active work
and waiting, with hours and %. Name the two biggest delays.

Mon 10:00  Ticket created by PM
Mon 11:30  Engineer asks: 404 or redirect? Pagination?
Tue 15:00  PM answers
... (15 more events, deploy next Tue 10:00)
```

```md
| Category                        | Hours | %   |
|---------------------------------|-------|-----|
| Active — coding                 | 5.0   | 10% |
| Active total                    | 15.5  | 32% |
| Waiting — PM answer             | 11.5  | 24% |
| Waiting — first code review     | 5.5   | 11% |
| Waiting — second code review    | 3.5   | 7%  |
| Waiting total                   | 32.5  | 68% |
[... other rows trimmed ...]
```

What the engineer did:

- Checked the total: Mon 10:00 to next Tue 10:00 is 48 working hours. Correct.
- Caught a slip: later the AI called one wait 5h, but its table said 6h. Lunch was the likely gap, so they added
  "skip 12:00–13:00" to the prompt.
- Ignored the advice to "use AI for coding". Halving 5 hours saves only 2.5.
- Found the real fix: ask all the questions on day one. Lesson 2.2.2 shows how AI can help with that.

## Watch out

- AI often gets time math wrong, especially weekends and lunch. Always check it.
- Tickets don't show thinking or meetings. Note what's missing.
- Use roles, not names, and keep private data out of the prompt.

## Try it

Do the five steps for one feature you shipped recently, or use SHIP-142. Done looks like: a table that adds up, and
one sentence naming the biggest delay.

<Answer label="What a good result looks like">
  Most people find waiting is bigger than working, and coding is a small row. Pick one small change, like "send all
  my questions within an hour of picking up a ticket", and measure again next month.
</Answer>

## Remember

- Measure where time goes before deciding where AI helps.
- Speeding up a small step gives a small win.
- Always check the AI's numbers.
